UNIT 01 · BUILD STRONG FOUNDATIONS

Numbers, fractions and percentages

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: numbers you use every day

A fraction is a way to cut something into equal parts: a pizza cut into 8 slices where you eat 3 is 3/8. A percentage is a fraction with 100 slices, so 25% means 25/100.

  • Numerator: the top number: how many parts you have.
  • Denominator: the bottom number: how many equal parts make the whole.
  • Percent: “out of 100”.

To compare or combine numbers, put them in the same form. To find a percentage of a number, change it to a decimal and multiply: 20% of 80 = 0.20 × 80 = 16.

Equivalent fractions

Multiplying or dividing the top and bottom by the same number keeps the value: 1/2 = 2/4 = 50/100. To simplify, divide both by their biggest common factor.

Adding and subtracting fractions

The denominators must match first. For 1/3 + 1/4 use 12: 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12. To multiply fractions, multiply straight across. To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply.

Percentage change

Percentage change = change ÷ original × 100%. A price rising from 50 to 60 is 10 ÷ 50 = 20% up.

Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100%
Common mistake: Always divide by the original value, not the new one.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

A $80 jacket is reduced by 15%. Find the sale price

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  2. Discount = 0.15 × 80 = 12.
  3. Sale price = 80 − 12 = $68.
  4. Shortcut: multiply by 0.85, because 100% − 15% = 85%.

UNIT 02 · USE LETTERS FOR UNKNOWNS

Algebra basics

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: using letters for numbers

In algebra a letter is a box that holds a number we do not know yet, or one that can change. If a pen costs p dollars, three pens cost 3p. Algebra is a short way to write rules that work for any number.

  • Variable: a letter standing for a number.
  • Term: a single piece, like 5x or 7.
  • Coefficient: the number multiplying the letter: in 5x it is 5.
  • Expression: terms combined, with no equals sign.

You can add or subtract only “like terms”, which have the same letter part. 3x + 2x = 5x, but 3x + 2 cannot be joined, just as 3 apples + 2 pears are not 5 of anything.

Simplifying

Collect like terms: 4a + 3b − a + 5b = 3a + 8b.

Expanding brackets

Multiply everything inside by the outside term: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12, and −2(x − 5) = −2x + 10.

Factorising

The reverse of expanding: pull out a common factor. 6x + 9 = 3(2x + 3).

Substitution

Replace letters with numbers. If x = 4, then 3x − 5 = 3 × 4 − 5 = 7.

a(b + c) = ab + ac
Common mistake: −2(x − 5) = −2x + 10, not −2x − 10: a negative times a negative is positive.

UNIT 03 · KEEP BOTH SIDES BALANCED

Linear equations and inequalities

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: balancing equations

An equation is a balanced scale. Whatever you do to one side you must do to the other. Solving means finding the number that makes both sides equal.

  • Equation: two expressions joined by =.
  • Solution: the value that makes it true.
  • Inverse operation: the operation that undoes another: + and −, × and ÷.
  • Inequality: uses <, >, ≤ or ≥ instead of =.

Undo operations in reverse order. For 3x + 5 = 20: subtract 5 from both sides to get 3x = 15, then divide by 3 to get x = 5. Always check by substituting back.

Unknowns on both sides

Move the letter terms to one side first. 5x − 2 = 3x + 8: subtract 3x to get 2x − 2 = 8, add 2 to get 2x = 10, so x = 5.

Equations with brackets

Expand first: 2(x + 3) = 14 becomes 2x + 6 = 14, so x = 4.

Inequalities

Solve like equations, but if you multiply or divide by a negative number, flip the sign: −2x < 6 gives x > −3. On a number line an open circle means “not included” and a filled one means “included”.

Word problems

Choose a letter, turn the sentence into an equation, solve, and answer in words.

Do the same to both sides
Common mistake: Forgetting to flip the inequality sign when dividing by a negative.

UNIT 04 · SHORTCUTS FOR HUGE AND TINY NUMBERS

Powers, roots and standard form

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: repeated multiplication

2³ means 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The small raised number is the power: how many times to multiply the base by itself. A square root undoes squaring: √25 = 5 because 5² = 25.

  • Base: the number being multiplied.
  • Exponent: the power: how many copies are multiplied.
  • Square root: the number that squares to give the original.
  • Standard form: a × 10ⁿ with 1 ≤ a < 10.

Learn the laws; they save a lot of work. Multiplying the same base: add the powers. Dividing: subtract. A power of a power: multiply.

Laws of powers

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ, aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ and (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ. For example 2³ × 2⁴ = 2⁷.

Zero and negative powers

a⁰ = 1 for any a ≠ 0, and a⁻ⁿ = 1 ÷ aⁿ. So 5⁻² = 1/25.

Roots and surds

√(ab) = √a × √b, so √50 = √25 × √2 = 5√2. A surd is a root that is not an exact fraction, like √2.

Standard form

Write very large or small numbers as a × 10ⁿ: 45 000 = 4.5 × 10⁴ and 0.0003 = 3 × 10⁻⁴.

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ · aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ · a⁰ = 1
Common mistake: (2 + 3)² is 25, not 2² + 3². Powers do not split over addition.

UNIT 05 · DRAW THE RULE

Linear functions and graphs

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: straight-line graphs

A graph is a picture of a rule. For every x on the horizontal axis, the rule gives a y on the vertical axis. A straight line means y changes by the same amount for each step in x, like a taxi fare that grows steadily with distance.

  • Coordinates: a pair (x, y) that locates a point.
  • Gradient: the steepness: rise ÷ run.
  • y-intercept: where the line crosses the y-axis.
  • Function: a rule that gives one output for each input.

Every straight line has the form y = mx + c. Here m is the gradient and c is the y-intercept. To draw it, mark c on the y-axis, then go right 1 and up m.

Finding the gradient

Take two points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂): gradient = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁). Uphill lines have a positive gradient and downhill lines a negative one.

Equation of a line

Given the gradient m and one point, substitute into y = mx + c to find c.

Parallel and perpendicular

Parallel lines have equal gradients. Perpendicular lines have gradients that multiply to −1, like 2 and −1/2.

Real-life meaning

In a taxi fare y = 0.5x + 2, the gradient 0.5 is the price per km and 2 is the starting charge.

y = mx + c · m = (y₂ − y₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)
Common mistake: Subtract the coordinates in the same order on the top and the bottom.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

  3. Answer: y = 2x + 1.

UNIT 06 · TWO CLUES, TWO UNKNOWNS

Systems of equations

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: solving two equations together

If I tell you x + y = 10, many pairs work: 3 and 7, 4 and 6. With a second clue, x − y = 2, only one pair fits: x = 6 and y = 4. Two equations solved together are called simultaneous equations.

  • Simultaneous equations: equations that must be true at the same time.
  • Substitution: replacing one letter with an expression.
  • Elimination: adding or subtracting equations to cancel a letter.
  • Solution: the pair (x, y) that fits both equations.

For elimination, make the number in front of one letter the same in both equations, then add or subtract to remove it. Solve for the other letter, then substitute back.

Elimination

For x + y = 10 and x − y = 2, add them: 2x = 12, so x = 6 and y = 4. If the coefficients differ, multiply an equation first so one pair matches.

Substitution

If y = 2x is given, put 2x in place of y in the other equation: x + 2x = 12 gives x = 4 and y = 8.

Graphs

Each equation is a line and the solution is where they cross. Parallel lines never cross, so they have no solution.

Word problems

Name two unknowns, write two equations from the story, and solve them.

Eliminate one letter, solve, substitute back
Common mistake: Find both letters and check the answer in both equations.

UNIT 07 · EQUATIONS WITH A SQUARE

Quadratics

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: curves and squares

A quadratic has an x² term. Its graph is a curve called a parabola, like the path of a thrown ball. Because of the square, a quadratic equation can have up to two solutions.

  • Quadratic: an expression of the form ax² + bx + c.
  • Root: an x-value that makes it equal zero.
  • Factorising: writing it as a product of brackets.
  • Discriminant: b² − 4ac: it tells you how many solutions there are.

If two numbers multiply to zero, one of them must be zero. So factorise to get (x + p)(x + q) = 0, then x = −p or x = −q.

Factorising x² + bx + c

Find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b. For x² + 5x + 6 the numbers are 2 and 3, so it factorises to (x + 2)(x + 3).

Solving by factorising

Move everything to one side so it equals zero, factorise, then set each bracket to zero.

The quadratic formula

For ax² + bx + c = 0, x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a. Use it when factorising is hard.

The discriminant

If b² − 4ac > 0 there are two solutions, if it equals 0 there is one, and if it is below 0 there are none.

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) ÷ 2a
Common mistake: Make the equation equal zero before you factorise.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Solve x² − 5x + 6 = 0

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Find numbers that multiply to 6 and add to −5: −2 and −3.
  2. Factorise: (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0.
  3. So x = 2 or x = 3.

UNIT 08 · THE RULES OF SHAPE

Angles, triangles and Pythagoras

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: shapes and their rules

Geometry is the maths of shape and space. Its power is in rules: once you know one angle, rules let you find others without measuring.

  • Angle: a turn measured in degrees (°).
  • Parallel lines: lines that never meet, always the same distance apart.
  • Hypotenuse: the longest side of a right-angled triangle, opposite the right angle.
  • Congruent: exactly the same shape and size.

Start every problem by marking what you know on the diagram. Then look for the rule that connects what you know to what you want.

Angle rules

Angles on a straight line add to 180°, around a point to 360°, and in a triangle to 180°. Vertically opposite angles are equal.

Parallel lines

A line crossing parallel lines makes equal corresponding angles and equal alternate angles (a Z shape). Co-interior angles (a C shape) add to 180°.

Polygons

The angles of a quadrilateral add to 360°. For an n-sided polygon, the interior angles add to (n − 2) × 180°.

Pythagoras’ theorem

In a right-angled triangle, a² + b² = c², where c is the hypotenuse. If a triangle’s sides fit this rule, it has a right angle.

a² + b² = c² · interior angles = (n − 2) × 180°
Common mistake: Only use Pythagoras in right-angled triangles, and find c² before taking the square root.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. The ladder is the hypotenuse: c = 5. One side is 4.
  2. a² + 4² = 5², so a² = 25 − 16 = 9.
  3. a = 3 m.

UNIT 09 · MEASURE SPACE

Perimeter, area, volume and circles

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: measuring space

Perimeter is the distance around a shape, like a fence. Area is the surface inside it, like grass to cover, measured in square units. Volume is the space inside a 3D object, measured in cubic units.

  • Perimeter: total length around the edge.
  • Area: surface inside, in cm², m² and so on.
  • Volume: space inside, in cm³, m³ and so on.
  • Radius: the distance from the centre of a circle to its edge; the diameter is twice the radius.

Always write the unit: squared for area, cubed for volume. For circles use π ≈ 3.14, or the π button on your calculator.

Area formulas

Rectangle: l × w. Triangle: ½ × base × height. Parallelogram: base × height. Trapezium: ½(a + b)h.

Circles

Circumference C = 2πr = πd and area A = πr². With r = 5: C ≈ 31.4 and A ≈ 78.5.

Prisms and cylinders

Volume = base area × height. A cylinder with r = 3 and h = 10 has V = π × 3² × 10 = 90π ≈ 283.

Cones, pyramids, spheres

Cone and pyramid: V = ⅓ × base area × height. Sphere: V = 4/3 πr³ and surface area 4πr².

A = πr² · C = 2πr · V = base area × height
Common mistake: Using the diameter where the formula needs the radius.

UNIT 10 · ANGLES AND SIDES

Trigonometry

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: angles and sides of right-angled triangles

If you know an angle of a right-angled triangle, the ratios of its sides never change, however big the triangle is. Trigonometry gives these fixed ratios names so you can find missing sides and angles.

  • Hypotenuse: the longest side, opposite the right angle.
  • Opposite: the side facing your chosen angle.
  • Adjacent: the side touching your angle, other than the hypotenuse.
  • Ratio: a comparison of two lengths by division.

Label the triangle first: hypotenuse, opposite and adjacent, relative to your angle. Then use SOH CAH TOA to pick the right ratio.

SOH CAH TOA

sin θ = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, cos θ = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, tan θ = opposite ÷ adjacent.

Finding a side

Choose the ratio containing the two sides involved. With an angle of 30° and hypotenuse 10, the opposite side x satisfies sin 30° = x ÷ 10, so x = 5.

Finding an angle

Use the inverse button. If tan θ = 0.75, then θ = tan⁻¹(0.75) ≈ 36.9°. Make sure your calculator is in degree mode.

Real-life uses

Heights of trees, slopes of roofs and angles of elevation are all solved with right-angled triangles.

sin θ = O ÷ H · cos θ = A ÷ H · tan θ = O ÷ A
Common mistake: A calculator in radian mode gives wrong answers. Set it to degrees.

UNIT 11 · MAKE SENSE OF DATA AND CHANCE

Statistics and probability

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: data and chance

Statistics turns lists of numbers into answers. Probability measures how likely something is, from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).

  • Mean: add all the values, then divide by how many.
  • Median: the middle value after sorting.
  • Mode: the most common value.
  • Range: largest minus smallest.

Pick the average that fits the data. If one extreme value drags the mean away from the rest, the median is usually fairer.

Averages

For 2, 3, 3, 5, 12: mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5, median = 3, mode = 3 and range = 10. The outlier 12 pulls the mean up.

Charts and spread

Bar charts compare categories, histograms show how continuous data is spread, and scatter graphs show whether two variables are related.

Probability

P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes. A fair die gives P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2. Also P(not A) = 1 − P(A).

Combined events

For independent events, multiply: P(two heads) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. For events that cannot both happen, add. Tree diagrams help organise this.

P(event) = favourable ÷ total · P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Common mistake: Sort the data before finding the median.

UNIT 12 · FIND THE PATTERN

Sequences and final review

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: patterns and putting it all together

A sequence is a list of numbers that follows a rule, like 3, 7, 11, 15. To use it, find the rule and write it as a formula that gives any term.

  • Term: one number in the sequence.
  • nth term: a formula giving the term in position n.
  • Arithmetic sequence: adds the same amount each time.
  • Common difference: the amount added each time.

For an arithmetic sequence, find the common difference d. The nth term is dn + c, where c = first term − d.

Arithmetic sequences

3, 7, 11, 15 has d = 4, so the nth term is 4n − 1. The 20th term is 4(20) − 1 = 79.

Geometric sequences

Multiply by the same ratio each time: 2, 6, 18, 54 has ratio 3 and nth term 2 × 3ⁿ⁻¹.

Quadratic sequences

If the second differences are constant, the nth term contains n². For 2, 6, 12, 20 the differences are 4, 6, 8, the second difference is 2, and the nth term is n² + n.

Your revision toolkit

Estimate first, check units, test answers by substituting, and draw a diagram when stuck.

nth term = a + (n − 1)d
Common mistake: Do not assume a pattern from two terms. Check at least three.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Find the nth term of 5, 8, 11, 14

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Common difference d = 3.
  2. The nth term is 3n + c. The first term gives 5 = 3(1) + c, so c = 2.
  3. nth term = 3n + 2.

The sum of an arithmetic sequence

To add 1 + 2 + … + 100, pair the first and last: 1 + 100 = 101, and there are 50 pairs, so the total is 5050. In general, sum = n/2 × (first + last).

Challenge. Find 3 + 7 + 11 + … + 39.

There are 10 terms (3 + 4(n − 1) = 39 gives n = 10). Sum = 10/2 × (3 + 39) = 210.
