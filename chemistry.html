UNIT 01 · WHAT EVERYTHING IS MADE OF

Matter and the particle model

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: stuff and particles

Chemistry is the study of matter: anything that has mass and takes up space. Air, water, your phone and you are all matter. Everything is made of tiny particles too small to see, and how they are arranged decides whether something is a solid, a liquid or a gas.

  • Matter: anything that has mass and takes up space.
  • Particle: a tiny piece of matter, such as an atom or a molecule.
  • Physical change: a change of state or shape where no new substance forms.
  • Property: a feature of a substance, such as its melting point.

Imagine particles as people. In a solid they stand in fixed seats and only wobble. In a liquid they move around each other like a crowd. In a gas they run freely across the whole room.

States of matter

Solids have a fixed shape and volume because their particles are tightly packed and vibrate in place. Liquids have a fixed volume but take the shape of their container. Gases have no fixed shape or volume because their particles are far apart and move quickly.

Changes of state

Melting turns a solid into a liquid, and boiling or evaporating turns a liquid into a gas. Condensing and freezing go the other way. Sublimation is a solid turning straight into a gas. Heating gives particles more energy so they can overcome the forces holding them together.

Pure substances and mixtures

A pure substance has only one kind of particle, like distilled water or gold. A mixture contains different substances that are not chemically joined, like sea water or air. Pure substances melt at one sharp temperature; mixtures melt over a range.

Elements, compounds and mixtures

An element has only one type of atom. A compound has two or more elements chemically joined in a fixed ratio, such as H₂O. A mixture can be separated by physical methods, but a compound needs a chemical reaction.

Solid → liquid → gas: more energy, more freedom
Common mistake: Dissolving is not melting. In a solution the solid spreads out among the liquid’s particles; it does not change state.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Why does a puddle disappear on a warm day?

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Water particles at the surface gain energy from the Sun.
  2. Some move fast enough to escape into the air: this is evaporation.
  3. The liquid becomes water vapour, a gas, so the puddle shrinks.

Why temperature stays flat while melting

When ice melts at 0 °C, the added heat energy is used to break the forces holding particles in place, not to speed them up, so the temperature stays constant until all the ice has melted. The same happens at the boiling point.

A pure substance has one kind of particle, so all the forces break at the same temperature. In a mixture the particles are held differently, so melting starts and finishes at different temperatures.

UNIT 02 · THE BUILDING BLOCKS

Atoms

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: the tiny building blocks

Every substance is made of atoms. They are so small that millions of them could sit side by side across a full stop. An atom has a tiny, dense centre called the nucleus, with electrons moving around it, and mostly empty space in between.

  • Proton: a positive particle in the nucleus.
  • Neutron: a neutral particle in the nucleus.
  • Electron: a tiny negative particle in shells around the nucleus.
  • Atomic number: the number of protons in an atom.

An atom has no overall charge because its protons (+) and electrons (−) are equal in number. The number of protons decides which element it is: 1 proton is hydrogen, 6 is carbon and 8 is oxygen.

Inside the atom

Protons have a relative mass of 1 and charge +1. Neutrons have mass 1 and charge 0. Electrons have almost no mass and charge −1. Protons and neutrons make up nearly all of an atom’s mass.

Atomic number and mass number

Atomic number = protons. Mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = mass number − atomic number. Carbon-12 has 6 protons, 6 neutrons and 6 electrons.

Electron shells

Electrons fill the shells closest to the nucleus first: 2 in the first, 8 in the second and 8 in the third (for the first 20 elements). Sodium, with 11 electrons, is 2,8,1.

Isotopes and ions

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, such as carbon-12 and carbon-14. An ion is an atom that has gained or lost electrons, so it carries a charge.

Neutrons = mass number − atomic number
Common mistake: Atomic number counts protons only. Do not add the neutrons.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Find the particles in a sodium atom (mass number 23, atomic number 11)

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Protons = atomic number = 11.
  2. Electrons = protons = 11, because the atom is neutral.
  3. Neutrons = 23 − 11 = 12.
  4. Electron arrangement: 2,8,1.

Isotopes of an element have the same number of electrons, and chemical behaviour depends on electrons. So carbon-12 and carbon-14 react in the same way, but carbon-14 is radioactive and is used to date ancient remains.

UNIT 03 · THE MAP OF THE ELEMENTS

The periodic table

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: a map of the elements

The periodic table is like a map. Elements are arranged by atomic number, and neighbours in the same column behave in similar ways, just as members of a family share traits.

  • Group: a vertical column of the table.
  • Period: a horizontal row of the table.
  • Metal: shiny, conducts heat and electricity, found on the left and middle.
  • Non-metal: usually dull and a poor conductor, found on the right.

The group number tells you the outer electrons: group 1 elements have 1 outer electron and group 7 elements have 7. The period number tells you how many shells are in use.

Metals and non-metals

Metals are usually shiny, malleable and good conductors. Non-metals are usually brittle as solids and poor conductors. A staircase line on the table separates them.

Group 1: alkali metals

Lithium, sodium and potassium are soft, and they react with water to make hydrogen and an alkaline solution. They become more reactive as you go down the group.

Group 7: halogens

Fluorine, chlorine, bromine and iodine have 7 outer electrons and are reactive non-metals. Reactivity decreases down the group, and a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one from its salt.

Group 0: noble gases

Helium, neon and argon have full outer shells, so they are very unreactive. They are used in balloons, lighting and welding.

Group number = outer electrons · Period number = shells
Common mistake: Group 1 gets more reactive going down, but group 7 gets less reactive going down.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Rubidium is in group 1, below potassium.
  2. Reactivity increases down group 1.
  3. So it reacts even more violently than potassium, making hydrogen gas and an alkaline hydroxide.

Why reactivity changes down a group

Each group 1 atom has 1 outer electron. Lower down, that electron is further from the nucleus and shielded by more shells, so it is lost more easily. In group 7, atoms need to gain an electron; further down the gained electron is held less strongly, so reactivity falls.

Challenge. Explain why potassium is more reactive than lithium.

UNIT 04 · HOW ATOMS JOIN

Chemical bonding

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: why atoms stick together

Most atoms are not stable alone. They join to get a full outer shell of electrons, like the noble gases. They can do this by giving electrons away, taking them, or sharing them.

  • Ion: an atom that has gained or lost electrons.
  • Ionic bond: the attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • Covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons.
  • Molecule: a group of atoms joined by covalent bonds.

Ask: is it a metal and a non-metal (ionic), two non-metals (covalent), or all metal atoms (metallic)? That one question decides almost everything about the bonding.

Ionic bonding

A metal atom gives electrons to a non-metal. Sodium gives 1 electron to chlorine, making Na⁺ and Cl⁻ that attract strongly in a giant lattice. Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct electricity when molten or dissolved, but not as solids.

Covalent bonding

Non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons. In water, oxygen shares with two hydrogens to make H₂O. Small molecules have low melting points because only weak forces between molecules need to be overcome.

Giant covalent structures

Diamond and silicon dioxide have millions of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds, so they melt at very high temperatures. Graphite also conducts electricity because it has free electrons.

Metallic bonding

Metal atoms release their outer electrons, leaving a lattice of positive ions in a “sea” of free electrons. This explains why metals conduct and can be bent into shape.

Metal + non-metal = ionic · Non-metal + non-metal = covalent
Common mistake: Ionic compounds do not conduct as solids because their ions cannot move. They conduct when molten or dissolved.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Show how magnesium oxide forms

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Magnesium (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons to become Mg²⁺.
  2. Oxygen (2,6) gains 2 electrons to become O²⁻.
  3. The opposite charges attract, forming MgO, an ionic compound.

Why graphite and diamond differ

Both are pure carbon. In diamond, each carbon bonds to four others in a rigid 3D lattice, which makes it extremely hard. In graphite, each carbon bonds to three others in layers that slide over each other, and the fourth electron is free to carry charge.

UNIT 05 · THE LANGUAGE OF CHEMISTRY

Formulae and equations

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: chemical writing

Chemists use symbols and numbers as a short, universal writing system. H₂O says: two hydrogen atoms joined to one oxygen atom. A chemical equation is a sentence that says what changes into what.

  • Symbol: one or two letters for an element, like Na or Cl.
  • Formula: shows the atoms in a compound, like CO₂.
  • Reactant: a substance at the start of a reaction.
  • Product: a substance made in a reaction.

Atoms are never created or destroyed in a reaction. So the same number of each type of atom must appear on both sides of the arrow. Balancing means putting big numbers in front of formulae until that is true.

Writing formulae

Small subscripts show the atoms in a molecule: CO₂ has 1 carbon and 2 oxygens. For ionic compounds, balance the charges: Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ need two chlorides, so the formula is MgCl₂.

Word equations

methane + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water. Reactants go on the left and products on the right.

Balancing equations

Count the atoms on each side. Change only the big numbers in front, never the small subscripts. H₂ + O₂ → H₂O becomes 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

State symbols

(s) is solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) dissolved in water. For example: Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g).

Atoms in = atoms out
Common mistake: Never change a subscript to balance an equation. H₂O and H₂O₂ are different substances.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Balance Mg + O₂ → MgO

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Count oxygen: 2 on the left and 1 on the right. Put 2 before MgO: Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.
  2. Now magnesium: 1 on the left and 2 on the right. Put 2 before Mg.
  3. Balanced: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO.

UNIT 06 · WHEN SUBSTANCES CHANGE

Chemical reactions

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: new substances

In a chemical reaction, old substances disappear and new ones appear. Burning wood, rusting iron and baking a cake are chemical reactions. The atoms are the same, but they are rearranged.

  • Reaction: a change that makes new substances.
  • Combustion: burning a fuel in oxygen.
  • Oxidation: gaining oxygen.
  • Catalyst: speeds up a reaction without being used up.

Look for clues: gas bubbles, a colour change, a solid forming, a temperature change, or light. These suggest a new substance has formed, but only a chemical test can be sure.

Signs of a reaction

Bubbles, a colour change, a solid appearing (a precipitate), a temperature change, light or a new smell. Physical changes like melting do not make new substances.

Combustion

A fuel burns in oxygen and releases energy. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon makes carbon dioxide and water. With too little oxygen you get carbon monoxide or soot, and carbon monoxide is poisonous.

Decomposition and displacement

Heating can break a compound down: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. In displacement, a more reactive element replaces a less reactive one: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.

Conservation of mass

The total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products because no atoms are lost. In an open container a gas can escape, making it look as though mass disappeared.

Mass of reactants = mass of products
Common mistake: Mass is not lost when something burns. The gases formed escape into the air.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

24 g of magnesium burns with 16 g of oxygen. What mass of magnesium oxide forms?

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Mass is conserved.
  2. Reactants: 24 + 16 = 40 g.
  3. Mass of magnesium oxide = 40 g.

Oxidation is gaining oxygen (or losing electrons) and reduction is losing oxygen (or gaining electrons). They always happen together and are called redox. In 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, magnesium is oxidised and oxygen is reduced.

UNIT 07 · COUNTING ATOMS BY WEIGHING

The mole and calculations

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: counting by weighing

You cannot count atoms one by one, so chemists count them by weighing, like counting rice by weighing a bag. One mole is a huge fixed number of particles: 6.02 × 10²³, called Avogadro’s number. A dozen is 12; a mole is 6.02 × 10²³.

  • Mole: the amount containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles.
  • Relative atomic mass (Aᵣ): the mass of an atom, found on the periodic table.
  • Relative formula mass (Mᵣ): the sum of the Aᵣ values in a formula.
  • Molar mass: the mass of 1 mole, in g/mol.

The molar mass in g/mol is the same number as the Mᵣ. So 1 mole of carbon (Aᵣ = 12) weighs 12 g, and 1 mole of water (Mᵣ = 18) weighs 18 g.

Relative formula mass

Add up the Aᵣ of every atom in the formula. For CO₂ with C = 12 and O = 16: 12 + 16 + 16 = 44.

Moles and mass

moles = mass ÷ molar mass. For 88 g of CO₂: 88 ÷ 44 = 2 mol.

Rearranging

mass = moles × molar mass, and molar mass = mass ÷ moles. A formula triangle helps you pick the right form.

Percentage composition

% of an element = (Aᵣ × number of atoms) ÷ Mᵣ × 100. In water, hydrogen is (2 × 1) ÷ 18 × 100 ≈ 11.1%.

Reacting masses

The equation shows mole ratios. 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O means 2 mol of hydrogen reacts with 1 mol of oxygen.

moles = mass ÷ molar mass
Common mistake: Use the molar mass of the whole formula, not one element, and keep the unit g/mol.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

How many moles are in 36 g of water? (H = 1, O = 16)

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Molar mass of H₂O = 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol.
  2. moles = 36 ÷ 18.
  3. Answer: 2 mol.

UNIT 08 · FROM LEMONS TO SOAP

Acids, bases and salts

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: sour and slippery

Acids taste sour, like lemon juice and vinegar. Bases and alkalis feel slippery, like soap. The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline something is. Never taste chemicals in a lab; these are only everyday examples.

  • Acid: a substance that releases H⁺ ions in water.
  • Alkali: a base that dissolves in water, releasing OH⁻ ions.
  • Indicator: a dye that changes colour in acid or alkali.
  • pH: a scale from 0 to 14 for acidity.

pH 7 is neutral, like pure water. Below 7 is acidic, and the lower the number the stronger the acid. Above 7 is alkaline.

Indicators and pH

Litmus is red in acid and blue in alkali. Universal indicator shows a rainbow: red for strong acid, green for neutral and purple for strong alkali.

Neutralisation

acid + alkali → salt + water. For example HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. The key change is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.

Acids with metals and carbonates

acid + metal → salt + hydrogen. acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide turns limewater cloudy.

Naming salts

Hydrochloric acid makes chlorides, sulfuric acid makes sulfates and nitric acid makes nitrates. Zinc and sulfuric acid make zinc sulfate.

acid + base → salt + water
Common mistake: pH 3 is not “slightly” acidic. Each pH step is ten times stronger, so pH 3 is far more acidic than pH 6.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Name the products when hydrochloric acid reacts with calcium carbonate

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  2. The salt from hydrochloric acid is a chloride: calcium chloride.
  3. Products: calcium chloride, water and carbon dioxide.

Strong and weak acids

A strong acid, such as hydrochloric acid, ionises fully in water. A weak acid, such as the ethanoic acid in vinegar, ionises only partly. Strength is not concentration: concentration is how much acid is dissolved, and strength is how much of it ionises.

Yes. Strength is about ionisation and concentration is about how much acid is present, so the two are independent.

UNIT 09 · HOW FAST, HOW HOT

Rates of reaction and energy

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: speed and heat of reactions

Some reactions are explosive and some take years. A reaction cannot happen unless particles crash into each other with enough energy. The more often and the harder they collide, the faster the reaction.

  • Rate: how fast reactants are used up or products are made.
  • Collision: two particles hitting each other.
  • Catalyst: speeds up a reaction without being used up.
  • Activation energy: the minimum energy needed to start a reaction.

To speed up a reaction, increase the number of successful collisions per second. You can raise the temperature, increase the concentration, increase the surface area or add a catalyst.

Collision theory

Particles must collide with at least the activation energy to react. Not every collision is successful.

Factors affecting rate

Higher temperature: particles move faster and collide harder and more often. Higher concentration or pressure: more particles in the same space. Smaller pieces: a larger surface area. A catalyst gives an easier route with a lower activation energy.

Measuring rate

rate = amount of product formed ÷ time. On a graph, a steeper line means a faster reaction, and the curve flattens when a reactant has run out.

Energy changes

Exothermic reactions release energy, so the temperature rises (combustion, neutralisation). Endothermic reactions absorb energy, so the temperature falls (thermal decomposition). Breaking bonds takes energy and making bonds releases it.

rate = amount of product ÷ time
Common mistake: A catalyst is not used up and does not change how much product forms. It only speeds up the reaction.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Why does powdered marble react faster than marble chips with acid?

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Powder has a much larger surface area.
  2. More particles are exposed to the acid at once.
  3. So there are more collisions per second and the rate increases.

UNIT 10 · WHICH METAL WINS

Metals and the reactivity series

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: ranking metals

Some metals, like potassium, react violently with water, while gold stays shiny for thousands of years. The reactivity series ranks metals from most to least reactive, and it predicts which reactions will happen.

  • Reactivity series: a list of metals from most to least reactive.
  • Displacement: a more reactive metal pushing a less reactive one out of its compound.
  • Ore: rock containing enough metal to be worth extracting.
  • Corrosion: slow damage to a metal by air and water.

Learn the order: potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, (carbon), zinc, iron, copper, silver, gold. Metals higher up lose electrons more easily and react more strongly.

Reactions of metals

Very reactive metals react with water to give a hydroxide and hydrogen. Less reactive ones react with acids. Copper, silver and gold do not react with dilute acids.

Displacement

Iron + copper sulfate → iron sulfate + copper. The iron is more reactive, so it takes the place of copper. A less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one.

Extracting metals

Metals below carbon, such as iron, are extracted by heating the ore with carbon: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Metals above carbon, such as aluminium, need electrolysis.

Rusting

Iron rusts when it is exposed to both oxygen and water. Prevent it with paint, oil, galvanising (a zinc coating) or sacrificial protection.

More reactive metal displaces less reactive metal
Common mistake: Carbon cannot extract metals above it in the series. Aluminium needs electrolysis.

Electrolysis

Electrolysis uses electricity to break down molten or dissolved ionic compounds. Positive ions go to the negative electrode (cathode) and gain electrons. Negative ions go to the positive electrode (anode) and lose electrons. Molten aluminium oxide gives aluminium at the cathode and oxygen at the anode.

UNIT 11 · SEPARATE WHAT IS MIXED

Mixtures, solutions and separation

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: sorting mixtures

A mixture can be separated because its substances are not chemically joined. To choose a method, ask: what is different about the substances? Their size, their boiling point, or how well they dissolve?

  • Solute: the substance that dissolves.
  • Solvent: the liquid it dissolves in.
  • Solution: a solute dissolved in a solvent.
  • Insoluble: does not dissolve.

Match the method to the difference. An insoluble solid in a liquid: filtration. A dissolved solid: evaporation or crystallisation. Liquids with different boiling points: distillation. Different dyes: chromatography.

Filtration and evaporation

Filtration removes an insoluble solid, like sand from water. Evaporating the liquid leaves a dissolved solid behind, like salt from sea water.

Distillation

Simple distillation separates a solvent from a solution: boil it, cool the vapour in a condenser and collect the pure liquid. Fractional distillation separates liquids with different boiling points, such as crude oil.

Chromatography

Dyes separate on paper because they dissolve in the solvent and stick to the paper by different amounts. Rf = distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent.

Solubility and concentration

Solubility is the maximum mass that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature. Most solids dissolve more when it is hotter. Concentration = mass ÷ volume, in g/dm³.

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent
Common mistake: Evaporation leaves the solute behind. Distillation collects the solvent.

UNIT 12 · THE CHEMISTRY OF FUELS AND PLASTICS

Carbon compounds and final review

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: why carbon is special

Carbon atoms can join to each other in long chains and rings, which makes millions of different compounds, from petrol to plastics to molecules in your body. Compounds of only carbon and hydrogen are called hydrocarbons.

  • Hydrocarbon: a compound of only carbon and hydrogen.
  • Alkane: a hydrocarbon with only single bonds.
  • Alkene: a hydrocarbon with a C=C double bond.
  • Polymer: a very long molecule made of many repeating units.

Alkanes follow CₙH₂ₙ₊₂: methane CH₄, ethane C₂H₆, propane C₃H₈, butane C₄H₁₀. Alkenes follow CₙH₂ₙ: ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆.

Crude oil and fractions

Crude oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons separated by fractional distillation. Short chains have low boiling points and are used as fuels like petrol. Long chains are thick and are used for bitumen.

Cracking

Large hydrocarbons are broken into smaller, more useful ones, producing alkenes: C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄.

Alkenes and polymers

Alkenes are unsaturated and turn bromine water colourless, but alkanes do not. Many ethene molecules join to form poly(ethene) by addition polymerisation.

Problems with plastics

Most plastics are made from crude oil and do not rot, because microbes cannot break their strong C–C bonds. They stay in the environment for a very long time, so recycling, reusing and developing biodegradable polymers all matter.
