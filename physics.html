UNIT 01 · BUILD THE LANGUAGE OF PHYSICS

Measurements and units

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: measuring things

Imagine a recipe that says “add some flour.” You could not repeat it. “Add 200 grams” works for everyone. Physics is the same: to describe nature so anyone can check it, we use a number and a unit together.

  • Quantity: something you can measure, like length, mass or time.
  • Unit: the agreed size you count in, like metres or seconds.
  • Prefix: a word in front of a unit that means a power of ten (kilo = 1000, milli = 1/1000).

Think of a unit as a ruler. A kilometre is a very long ruler, so you need few of them; a metre is shorter, so you need more. Changing to a smaller unit makes the number bigger (multiply). Changing to a bigger unit makes the number smaller (divide).

Learning goals

  • Explain what a physical quantity is and why units matter.
  • Recognize common SI units and symbols.
  • Convert simple measurements between metric units.
  • Estimate and measure carefully, including uncertainty.

Physical quantities

A physical quantity is a property that can be measured and expressed as a number and a unit. For example, 2.5 seconds describes a time interval; 2.5 by itself is incomplete. Measurements let scientists compare results and communicate clearly.

Base units and derived units

The International System of Units (SI) gives scientists a shared system. Common base units include metre (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature, and ampere (A) for electric current. Some units are derived from others: speed uses metres per second (m/s), and force uses newtons (N).

Prefixes and conversions

Metric prefixes show powers of ten. Kilo- means 1000, centi- means one hundredth, and milli- means one thousandth. Therefore 1 km = 1000 m, 1 m = 100 cm, and 1 m = 1000 mm. Convert by multiplying or dividing by the correct factor.

Accuracy and uncertainty

Every real measurement has some uncertainty. A ruler marked in millimetres usually lets you read length more precisely than a ruler marked only in centimetres. Record sensible precision: do not write more decimal places than the measuring tool supports.

1 km = 1000 m · 1 m = 100 cm · 1 min = 60 s
Common mistake: Do not change the number without changing the unit. 3 km is not 3 m; it is 3000 m.

Significant figures and scientific notation

Significant figures show how precisely a value is known: 0.0450 m has three. Scientific notation keeps very large and very small numbers readable by writing them as a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten, such as 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s for the speed of light. When you multiply or divide measurements, give the answer to the fewest significant figures in your data.

UNIT 02 · DESCRIBE HOW OBJECTS TRAVEL

Motion and speed

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: how things move

On a road trip you do not drive at the same speed all the time, yet you can still say “we covered 240 km in 3 hours.” Speed answers one question: how much distance is covered in each unit of time?

  • Distance: how far you travelled along the path.
  • Displacement: how far you are from the start in a straight line, with direction.
  • Speed: distance divided by time, for example metres per second.

Use the triangle idea: speed = distance ÷ time. Cover the thing you want to find. If you want distance, multiply speed by time. If you want time, divide distance by speed. On a distance–time graph, a steeper line means faster, and a flat line means standing still.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish distance from displacement in simple situations.
  • Calculate average speed using distance and time.
  • Rearrange the speed formula to find distance or time.
  • Read and interpret simple distance–time graphs.

Distance and displacement

Distance is the total length of the route travelled. Displacement is the change in position from start to finish, including direction. If you walk 100 m around a track and return to the start, your distance is 100 m but your displacement is zero.

Average speed

Average speed tells you how much distance is covered per unit of time. It does not mean the object moved at that speed every second; it averages the whole journey.

Units and rearranging formulas

Average speed = distance ÷ time. In symbols, v = d/t. Rearranging gives d = vt and t = d/v. Use matching units: metres and seconds give m/s; kilometres and hours give km/h.

Distance–time graphs

On a distance–time graph, time is usually on the horizontal axis and distance on the vertical axis. A steeper line means a greater speed. A horizontal line means the distance from the starting point is not changing, so the object is stopped.

v = d ÷ t · d = v × t · t = d ÷ v
Common mistake: Do not divide time by distance. Speed is distance divided by time.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

A cyclist travels 150 m in 30 s

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Write the formula: speed = distance ÷ time.
  2. Substitute: speed = 150 m ÷ 30 s.
  3. Calculate: speed = 5 m/s.
  4. Check the unit: metres divided by seconds gives m/s.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Average speed versus instantaneous speed

Average speed describes a whole journey, while instantaneous speed is the speed at one moment, like the reading on a car speedometer. On a distance–time graph, instantaneous speed is the steepness of the line at a single point. Average speed is always total distance divided by total time, never the average of two speeds.

Challenge. A car drives 60 km at 30 km/h, then 60 km at 60 km/h. What is its average speed?

Total distance 120 km. Time = 2 h + 1 h = 3 h. Average speed = 120 ÷ 3 = 40 km/h (not 45 km/h).

UNIT 03 · UNDERSTAND CHANGES IN VELOCITY

Acceleration

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: changing speed

When you press a car’s accelerator, the speedometer climbs. Acceleration is not “being fast.” It is how quickly your velocity changes. A car moving fast at a steady rate has zero acceleration.

  • Velocity: speed with a direction.
  • Acceleration: change in velocity divided by the time taken.
  • m/s²: metres per second, every second.

An acceleration of 2 m/s² means the speed grows by 2 m/s each second: 2, 4, 6, 8… Slowing down is acceleration in the opposite direction, which we write as a negative number. Always subtract the starting velocity from the final velocity first, then divide by time.

Learning goals

  • Explain the difference between speed and velocity.
  • Calculate average acceleration.
  • Use acceleration units correctly.
  • Describe acceleration on a velocity–time graph.

Speed and velocity

Speed tells how fast an object moves. Velocity also includes direction. A car moving at 10 m/s east and one moving at 10 m/s west have the same speed but different velocities.

What acceleration means

Acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes. An object accelerates if it speeds up, slows down, or changes direction. Average acceleration equals change in velocity divided by the time taken.

Using the formula

a = (v − u)/t, where u is initial velocity, v is final velocity, and t is the time interval. The SI unit is m/s². This means velocity changes by that many metres per second each second.

Velocity–time graphs

The slope (gradient) of a velocity–time graph represents acceleration. A flat line means constant velocity and zero acceleration. A line sloping upward means positive acceleration in the chosen direction.

a = (v − u) ÷ t
Common mistake: A negative acceleration is not automatically the same as slowing down in every situation; interpret the sign using the chosen direction and velocity.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

A scooter speeds up from 2 m/s to 14 m/s in 4 s

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Write a = (v − u)/t.
  2. Substitute: a = (14 − 2) ÷ 4.
  3. Calculate: a = 12 ÷ 4 = 3 m/s².
  4. Interpretation: velocity increases by 3 m/s each second on average.

PART 3 · GUIDED PRACTICE

Your turn

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

The equations of uniform acceleration

When acceleration is constant, three relationships link initial velocity u, final velocity v, acceleration a, time t and displacement s: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², and v² = u² + 2as. Choose the equation that contains the three quantities you know and the one you want. Always decide on a positive direction first and keep to it.

Challenge. A cyclist starts from rest and accelerates at 2 m/s² for 5 s. Find the final speed and the distance travelled.

v = 0 + 2 × 5 = 10 m/s. s = ½ × 2 × 5² = 25 m.

UNIT 04 · FIND OUT WHY MOTION CHANGES

Forces and Newton’s laws

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: pushes and pulls

A force is a push or a pull. You cannot see forces, only what they do: start things moving, stop them, speed them up or change their shape. Newton described three simple rules for them.

  • Newton’s 1st law: things keep doing what they are doing unless a net force acts, like a book staying on a table.
  • Newton’s 2nd law: F = ma: the same push speeds up an empty shopping trolley more than a full one.
  • Newton’s 3rd law: forces come in pairs: when you push a wall, the wall pushes back on you.

Always ask: what are all the forces on this object? Add them with direction to find the resultant (net) force. Balanced forces give no change in motion. Unbalanced forces cause acceleration. Friction is the force that resists sliding between surfaces.

Learning goals

  • Identify common forces and draw simple force diagrams.
  • Distinguish balanced and unbalanced forces.
  • Use Newton’s second law F = ma.
  • Explain Newton’s first and third laws with examples.

Forces are interactions

A force is a push or pull. Forces are measured in newtons (N) and have both size and direction. Common forces include weight (gravity), friction, air resistance, tension, normal contact force, and thrust.

Resultant force

The resultant force is the overall effect of all forces on an object. Equal forces in opposite directions are balanced, so the resultant is zero. Unbalanced forces cause acceleration. Zero resultant force does not mean an object must be stationary; it may move at constant velocity.

Newton’s first and second laws

The first law says an object remains at rest or moves at constant velocity unless a resultant external force acts on it. The second law gives the relationship F = ma: resultant force equals mass times acceleration.

Newton’s third law

When object A exerts a force on object B, object B exerts an equal and opposite force on A. These forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other on one object.

F = m × a · 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²
Common mistake: Newton’s third-law forces act on different objects. Do not draw them as cancelling forces on one object.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Find the force needed to accelerate a 4 kg cart at 2 m/s²

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Write the formula: F = ma.
  2. Substitute mass and acceleration: F = 4 × 2.
  3. Calculate: F = 8 N.
  4. This is the resultant force, not necessarily one individual force if several forces act.

PART 3 · GUIDED PRACTICE

Your turn

Try each question on paper first. Use the reveal buttons only after you have attempted an answer.

Try it yourself 2. A book rests on a table. Are the vertical forces balanced?

Yes. The table’s upward normal force balances the book’s weight if it remains at rest.

Try it yourself 3. When you push a wall, what is the third-law partner force?

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Free-body diagrams and terminal velocity

A free-body diagram shows one object and every force acting on it as arrows, with length showing size. Adding the arrows gives the resultant force. A falling object speeds up until air resistance equals its weight; the resultant force is then zero and it falls at a steady terminal velocity.

Challenge. A 1200 kg car has an engine force of 3000 N and friction of 600 N. Find its acceleration.

Resultant = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N. a = F ÷ m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2 m/s².

UNIT 05 · COMPARE MATTER AND GRAVITATIONAL FORCE

Mass, weight and density

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: how heavy and how packed

A bag of feathers and a bag of bricks can be the same size, yet one is far heavier. Three ideas explain this: mass, weight and density, and beginners often mix them up.

  • Mass: how much matter is in an object, in kilograms; it never changes.
  • Weight: the pull of gravity on that mass, in newtons; it changes with the planet.
  • Density: mass per unit volume: how tightly the matter is packed.

On Earth, g = 10 N/kg means every kilogram is pulled down with about 10 newtons, so weight = mass × g. Density = mass ÷ volume. Bricks have a high density because a lot of mass is packed into a small volume. Always keep kilograms (mass) and newtons (weight) separate.

Learning goals

  • Explain how mass differs from weight.
  • Calculate weight using W = mg.
  • Calculate density using mass and volume.
  • Use correct units in calculations.

Mass

Mass measures the amount of matter in an object and is measured in kilograms (kg). In introductory physics, an object's mass does not change just because it moves to another planet or moon.

Weight

Weight is the gravitational force acting on a mass. Near Earth's surface, W = mg, where g is gravitational field strength, about 9.8 N/kg (often approximated as 10 N/kg in school calculations unless instructed otherwise). Weight is measured in newtons.

Density

Density tells us how much mass is packed into a volume. Density = mass ÷ volume. A compact block and a larger block can have the same mass but different densities if their volumes differ.

Units and fair comparisons

Common density units are kg/m³ and g/cm³. Check that the mass and volume units match the density unit you plan to use. Objects float or sink in a fluid depending on their average density compared with the fluid, along with buoyancy effects.

Weight: W = mg · Density: ρ = m ÷ V
Common mistake: Kilograms measure mass, not weight. Weight is a force measured in newtons.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Calculate the weight of a 6 kg bag using g = 10 N/kg

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Write W = mg.
  2. Substitute W = 6 kg × 10 N/kg.
  3. Calculate W = 60 N.
  4. The mass is 6 kg; the weight is 60 N. They are different quantities.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Why weight changes but mass does not

Weight is W = mg, and g depends on where you are: about 10 N/kg on Earth and about 1.6 N/kg on the Moon. Density also explains floating and sinking. An object floats in a fluid when its average density is lower than the fluid's, which is why a steel ship floats even though solid steel sinks.

Challenge. An astronaut has a mass of 50 kg. What is their weight on the Moon, where g = 1.6 N/kg?

W = mg = 50 × 1.6 = 80 N.

UNIT 06 · TRACK ENERGY TRANSFERS

Work, energy and power

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: energy, work and power

Energy is the ability to make things happen: heat a room, lift a box, light a lamp. It cannot be made or destroyed, only moved between stores. Work is the energy transferred when a force moves something.

  • Work: force × distance moved in the direction of the force, in joules.
  • Power: how fast energy is transferred, in watts (1 W = 1 J per second).
  • Efficiency: the fraction of input energy that does the job you wanted.

Carry ten boxes upstairs slowly or quickly: the work is the same, but the quick trip needs more power. No machine is 100% efficient because some energy always spreads out as heat. Efficiency = useful output ÷ total input.

Learning goals

  • Describe work as energy transferred by a force.
  • Calculate work done in simple cases.
  • Recognize kinetic and gravitational potential energy.
  • Calculate power as the rate of energy transfer.

Work done

In a simple case, work is done when a force moves an object in the direction of that force. Work done = force × distance moved in the force's direction. The unit is the joule (J), equal to one newton metre.

Energy stores and transfers

Kinetic energy is associated with motion. Gravitational potential energy is associated with an object's position in a gravitational field. Energy can also be stored chemically, thermally, elastically, and in other forms. Energy is transferred between stores; it is not created or destroyed.

Power

Power tells how quickly work is done or energy is transferred. Power = energy transferred ÷ time. One watt (W) is one joule per second. Two devices may transfer the same energy, but the more powerful one does so in less time.

Efficiency

Not all input energy becomes useful output energy. Efficiency = useful output energy ÷ total input energy × 100%. Energy that spreads into the surroundings is often described as dissipated.

Work = force × distance · Power = energy ÷ time
Common mistake: Power is not the same as energy. Energy is measured in joules; power is measured in watts.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

A 10 N force pushes a box 4 m

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Use work = force × distance.
  2. Substitute: W = 10 N × 4 m.
  3. Work done = 40 J.
  4. If that work is done in 5 s, average power = 40 J ÷ 5 s = 8 W.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Kinetic energy, gravitational energy and conservation

Moving objects store kinetic energy, KE = ½mv². Raised objects store gravitational potential energy, GPE = mgh. Energy is never created or destroyed, only transferred between stores. When an object falls without friction, GPE lost equals KE gained, so you can find its speed from its height.

Challenge. A 2 kg ball is dropped from 5 m (g = 10 N/kg). Find its GPE at the start and its speed just before landing.

GPE = 2 × 10 × 5 = 100 J. All of it becomes KE: ½ × 2 × v² = 100, so v² = 100 and v = 10 m/s.

UNIT 07 · UNDERSTAND FORCE OVER AREA

Pressure and fluids

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: pressure

A person wearing snowshoes stays on top of snow, but someone in high heels sinks. Their weight is similar, yet the force is spread over different areas. Pressure measures how concentrated a force is.

  • Pressure: force ÷ area, measured in pascals (Pa).
  • Fluid: a liquid or a gas, anything that can flow.
  • Atmospheric pressure: the weight of the air above us pressing on everything.

Smaller area means higher pressure, which is why knives are sharp. In liquids, pressure grows with depth, which is why your ears feel it when diving. In gases, pressure comes from billions of tiny particles bouncing off the container walls.

Learning goals

  • Calculate pressure using P = F/A.
  • Explain why contact area affects pressure.
  • Describe how liquid pressure changes with depth.
  • Recognize pressure in gases and everyday situations.

Pressure in solids

Pressure describes how much force acts per unit area. Pressure = force ÷ area. Its SI unit is the pascal (Pa), equal to one newton per square metre (N/m²).

Why area matters

For the same force, a smaller contact area creates greater pressure. A sharp knife has a narrow edge, so the force is concentrated over a small area. Snowshoes spread a person's weight over a larger area and reduce pressure on snow.

Pressure in liquids

Liquid pressure increases with depth because deeper points have more liquid above them. At the same depth in the same stationary liquid, pressure is the same in every direction. Liquid pressure also depends on the liquid's density and gravitational field strength.

Gas pressure

Gas particles move randomly and collide with container walls. These collisions exert force over the wall area, creating pressure. Heating a gas in a sealed rigid container can increase pressure because particles move faster and collide more often and more forcefully.

Pressure = force ÷ area (P = F/A)
Common mistake: When calculating pressure, divide force by area—not multiply. Make sure area is in square metres for pressure in pascals.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

A 100 N force acts on an area of 2 m²

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. Use P = F/A.
  2. Substitute P = 100 N ÷ 2 m².
  3. Pressure = 50 Pa.
  4. If the same force acted on 1 m², the pressure would be 100 Pa—twice as large.

PART 3 · GUIDED PRACTICE

Your turn

Try each question on paper first. Use the reveal buttons only after you have attempted an answer.

Try it yourself 2. Why do snowshoes help a person avoid sinking into snow?

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Pressure in liquids and hydraulic systems

Pressure in a liquid depends on depth, density and gravity: p = ρgh. It does not depend on the shape of the container. Liquids are nearly incompressible, so a pressure applied at one point is transmitted everywhere in the fluid. Hydraulic jacks use this to turn a small force on a small piston into a large force on a large piston.

Challenge. Find the pressure due to water at a depth of 5 m (ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 10 N/kg).

p = ρgh = 1000 × 10 × 5 = 50 000 Pa.

UNIT 08 · EXPLORE THERMAL ENERGY

Heat and temperature

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: heat and temperature

Everything is made of tiny particles that are always moving. Temperature tells you how energetic that motion is on average. Heat is the energy that flows from a hotter place to a cooler one.

  • Temperature: how hot something is, in °C or kelvin.
  • Thermal energy: the total energy of all the particles.
  • Change of state: melting, boiling, freezing or condensing.

A bath of warm water has less temperature than a cup of boiling tea, but more thermal energy because there are far more particles. Energy travels three ways: conduction (through touching particles), convection (moving fluid) and radiation (waves, even through space). During melting or boiling the temperature stays steady while energy breaks particle bonds.

Learning goals

  • Distinguish temperature from thermal energy.
  • Describe conduction, convection, and radiation.
  • Explain heating and cooling in everyday examples.
  • Understand changes of state at a basic level.

Temperature and thermal energy

Temperature tells how hot or cold something is and is linked to the average energy of particles' random motion. Thermal energy depends on factors including the substance, its mass, and its temperature. A large warm bath may contain more thermal energy than a small hot cup.

Conduction

Conduction transfers energy through particle interactions without bulk movement of the material. In metals, free electrons also transfer energy efficiently, which is why metals are generally good thermal conductors.

Convection

Convection occurs in liquids and gases. When a region is heated, it often expands and becomes less dense, so it rises while cooler, denser fluid sinks. This circulation transfers thermal energy.

Radiation and changes of state

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic radiation, mainly infrared at everyday temperatures, and can travel through a vacuum. During melting or boiling, energy can change the arrangement of particles without increasing temperature until the change of state is complete.

Thermal energy transfers from warmer regions to cooler regions
Common mistake: Heat is energy being transferred because of a temperature difference; temperature itself is not a measure of total thermal energy.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

Why does a metal spoon get hot in tea?

Follow the method carefully. Cover the next step and try to predict it before reading on.

  1. The tea is hotter than the spoon, so thermal energy transfers from tea to spoon.
  2. Energy travels through the metal by conduction.
  3. The spoon's temperature rises as its particles and electrons transfer energy.
  4. A wooden or plastic handle is often used because these materials conduct heat less effectively than metal.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Specific heat capacity

Different materials need different amounts of energy to warm up. The specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C. The relationship is Q = mcΔT. Water has an unusually high value, about 4200 J/(kg·°C), which is why it warms slowly and is useful for heating and cooling systems.

Challenge. How much energy heats 2 kg of water by 10 °C (c = 4200 J/(kg·°C))?

Q = mcΔT = 2 × 4200 × 10 = 84 000 J.

UNIT 09 · SEE HOW WAVES CARRY ENERGY

Waves and sound

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: waves

At a stadium, a “wave” travels around the crowd, yet nobody leaves their seat. A physics wave is the same: it moves energy from place to place without carrying the material along.

  • Wavelength (λ): the distance of one full wave, in metres.
  • Frequency (f): how many waves pass each second, in hertz.
  • Amplitude: the maximum displacement from rest; bigger means more energy.

In a transverse wave the vibration is at right angles to the direction of travel (light, water ripples). In a longitudinal wave it is back and forth along the direction (sound). Wave speed = frequency × wavelength. High frequency sounds have a high pitch; big amplitude sounds are loud.

Learning goals

  • Identify amplitude, wavelength, frequency, and period.
  • Use v = fλ for wave speed.
  • Distinguish transverse and longitudinal waves.
  • Explain how sound travels and how pitch differs from loudness.

What a wave does

A wave transfers energy from place to place without a net transfer of matter overall. A floating leaf may bob as a water wave passes; it does not travel all the way with the wave.

Wave features

Amplitude is the maximum displacement from the resting position. Wavelength (λ) is the distance between matching points on neighbouring waves. Frequency (f) is the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz). Period is the time for one complete wave.

Wave speed

Wave speed = frequency × wavelength, or v = fλ. If frequency is measured in hertz and wavelength in metres, wave speed is measured in m/s. For a given wave in a fixed medium, changing frequency also changes wavelength so that speed remains determined by the medium.

Sound

Sound is a longitudinal mechanical wave: particles vibrate parallel to the direction the wave travels. Sound needs a medium, so it cannot travel through a vacuum. Higher frequency usually means higher pitch; greater amplitude is associated with a louder sound.

Wave speed = frequency × wavelength (v = fλ)
Common mistake: Frequency and amplitude describe different things: frequency relates to pitch for sound; amplitude relates to loudness.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Echoes and the speed of sound

Sound travels at roughly 343 m/s in air at room temperature, faster in liquids and fastest in solids because the particles are closer together. Echoes are reflections of sound. Because the sound travels to the surface and back, the one-way distance is half of the total distance travelled.

Challenge. You shout and hear an echo after 2 s. How far away is the reflecting wall? (Speed of sound = 343 m/s.)

Total distance = 343 × 2 = 686 m. The wall is half of that: 343 m away.

UNIT 10 · UNDERSTAND CURRENT, VOLTAGE AND RESISTANCE

Electricity and circuits

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: electric circuits

Picture water in a closed loop of pipes. A pump pushes the water, narrow pipes resist it, and the flow is what you measure. Electricity behaves the same way with charge.

  • Current (I): flow of charge, in amperes; like water flow.
  • Voltage (V): the push from the cell or battery, in volts; like the pump.
  • Resistance (R): how hard the path is to flow through, in ohms.

Ohm’s law links them: V = I × R. In a series circuit there is one path, so the current is the same everywhere. In a parallel circuit there are branches, so the current splits but each branch gets the full voltage. A circuit must be a complete loop or nothing flows.

Learning goals

  • Identify the parts of a simple circuit.
  • Explain current, potential difference, and resistance.
  • Use Ohm’s law V = IR.
  • Distinguish series and parallel circuits safely.

A complete circuit

A simple circuit can include a cell or battery, wires, a switch, and components such as a lamp or resistor. A complete conducting path is needed for steady current. A switch opens or closes the path.

Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. It is measured in amperes (A). In a simple series circuit, current is the same through each component because there is only one path.

Potential difference and resistance

Potential difference (voltage), measured in volts (V), describes energy transferred per unit charge between two points. Resistance, measured in ohms (Ω), describes how strongly a component opposes current. For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, V = IR.

Series and parallel

In a series circuit, components share one path and the supply voltage is divided between them. In a parallel circuit, components have separate branches; the potential difference across each branch is the same as across the supply, and current splits between branches.

V = I × R · I = V ÷ R · R = V ÷ I
Common mistake: Never experiment with household mains electricity. Use only safe, low-voltage classroom circuits and follow a teacher's instructions.

PART 2 · TEACHER EXAMPLE

  2. Rearrange to I = V/R.
  3. Substitute: I = 12 V ÷ 4 Ω.
  4. Current = 3 A.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Electrical power and combining resistors

Electrical power is P = IV, the energy transferred per second. Resistors in series add: R = R₁ + R₂. In parallel, the total resistance is smaller than the smallest branch, because charge has more paths to follow. Household sockets are wired in parallel so each device receives the full supply voltage.

Challenge. A 12 W lamp runs from a 6 V supply. What current does it draw?

I = P ÷ V = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A.

UNIT 11 · EXPLORE REFLECTION AND REFRACTION

Light and optics

Work through the sections in order and pause to answer the practice questions before revealing the solutions.

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: light

Light travels in straight lines. We see an object when light from it enters our eyes. When light meets a surface or a new material, three things can happen: it bounces, bends, or is absorbed.

  • Reflection: light bouncing off a surface; the angle in equals the angle out.
  • Refraction: light bending when it changes speed, such as entering water.
  • Normal: an imaginary line at 90° to the surface; all angles are measured from it.

Imagine a shopping trolley rolling from pavement onto grass at an angle: one wheel slows first, so the trolley turns. Light does the same when it enters glass. A converging lens bends rays together to a focus; a diverging lens spreads them out. White light splits into colours because each colour bends by a different amount.

Learning goals

  • Explain reflection using the normal line.
  • Apply the law of reflection.
  • Describe refraction when light enters a new medium.
  • Recognize how lenses and the spectrum work.

Light travels

Light is an electromagnetic wave and can travel through a vacuum. In a uniform transparent medium, it travels in straight lines. Shadows form when an opaque object blocks light.

Reflection

Reflection happens when light bounces from a surface. Draw a normal—a line perpendicular to the surface at the point where the ray hits. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection, and both angles are measured from the normal.

Refraction

Refraction is a change in direction when light crosses a boundary and its speed changes. Light entering a medium where it travels more slowly bends towards the normal; entering a medium where it travels faster bends away from the normal. A ray hitting straight along the normal changes speed but does not bend.

Lenses and colour

A converging lens brings parallel rays together; a diverging lens spreads them out. White light contains a range of colours, and a prism can separate them because different wavelengths refract by different amounts.

Angle of incidence = angle of reflection
Common mistake: Angles of incidence and reflection are measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.

GO DEEPER · EXTENSION

Snell's law and total internal reflection

The refractive index n = c ÷ v compares the speed of light in a vacuum with its speed in a material. Snell's law, n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂, links the angles. When light inside glass meets the boundary at an angle larger than the critical angle, it reflects completely. Optical fibres use total internal reflection to carry data as light pulses.

Challenge. Glass has a refractive index of 1.5. What is the speed of light in it? (c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.)

v = c ÷ n = 3.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.5 = 2.0 × 10⁸ m/s.

Revision and final test

PART 1 · LEARN THE THEORY

Start from zero: putting it all together

The final unit is about using everything you have learned. Most mistakes come not from missing physics, but from rushing: forgetting units, mixing up formulas or skipping a check.

  • Speed: v = d ÷ t.
  • Force: F = m × a, and weight W = m × g.
  • Energy and power: work = F × d, power = energy ÷ time.
  • Pressure, waves, circuits: p = F ÷ A, v = f × λ, V = I × R.

Use the same routine every time: write what you know with units, write the unknown, pick the formula, rearrange, substitute, calculate, and check that the unit and size make sense. If you are stuck, work out which unit you need and find the formula that produces it.

Learning goals

  • Choose the right formula for a problem.
  • Use units and show calculation steps.
  • Explain a concept in your own words.
  • Identify topics that need more revision.

A reliable problem-solving routine

Read the question twice. Write down what is given and what you need to find. Sketch a diagram if it helps. Choose a formula that connects the quantities, convert units if needed, substitute values, calculate, and write the final answer with its unit.

Check whether your answer makes sense

Estimate before calculating. A walking speed of 500 m/s is unreasonable; a mass of a school bag written as 6 N probably confuses mass and weight. Units can reveal mistakes before you finish.

Explain, don't only memorize

Try to explain why a result happens. For example, a smaller contact area increases pressure because the same force acts on each square metre more strongly. Explanations help you apply ideas to new situations.
